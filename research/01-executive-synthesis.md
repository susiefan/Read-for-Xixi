# Executive synthesis

## What the evidence supports

1. **Daily, enjoyable shared reading is worth encouraging.** The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends literacy promotion from infancy and emphasizes joyful, interactive reading embedded in family routines. It does not prescribe a universal one-hour daily minimum. Its age-specific guidance for 18–23 months gives 10–15 minutes as a practical bedtime routine example.

2. **Interaction quality matters more than passive narration.** Responsive turn-taking, following the child's attention, pausing, expanding the child's contribution, and dialogic reading are better supported than simply increasing adult word count or playing narration.

3. **Shared-reading interventions have positive but generally small child-language effects.** A 2020 meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials reported effects on expressive language (`d=0.41`) and receptive language (`d=0.26`) and a much larger effect on caregiver book-sharing competence (`d=1.01`). A stricter 2019 meta-analysis found a smaller overall language effect (`g=0.194`) and essentially no advantage against active controls. The defensible conclusion is “promising and useful,” not “transformational by itself.”

4. **Parents really do report fatigue, time constraints, limited confidence, access problems, and child disengagement.** A 2025 meta-synthesis of nine qualitative studies identified these as repeated barriers to shared book reading. This validates the broad problem, though Read-for-Xixi must still validate its specific target segment.

5. **Teaching baby signs has not been shown to create lasting spoken-language gains in typically developing hearing children.** The best systematic review found no robust language advantage after 24 months and no evidence of harm. Signs can still have value as an additional visual language, a creative medium, and a way to enrich shared storytelling.

6. **Signing content cannot safely be generated without expert control.** Sign languages encode meaning through handshape, location, orientation, motion, torso, gaze, and facial/nonmanual features. Current avatar research repeatedly identifies intelligibility and linguistic fidelity problems. A prevalidated clip library is safer than open-ended text-to-sign generation for V1.

7. **The product should reduce caregiver effort, not remove the caregiver.** Passive screen narration would undercut the best-supported mechanism: contingent parent-child interaction. The avatar should facilitate brief turns and offline play, and it may offer a low-energy mode when the parent is exhausted.

## Assumptions that did not survive research unchanged

### “One hour of reading per day”

Not supported as a universal AAP recommendation. AAP recommends reading together from infancy and making it a regular routine. The specific HealthyChildren guidance for 18–23 months suggests 10–15 minutes. Some literacy campaigns use 15 minutes daily.

The founder has nevertheless selected **60 minutes of daily reading engagement** as a product requirement. This must be described as a family/product goal rather than a universal medical mandate. The product can accumulate the hour across screen-supported stories, physical books, conversation, movement, and off-screen play. Current evidence does not establish a linear rule that every additional minute produces proportionally better outcomes.

### “Hiring a sign-language person costs $70,000 per year”

Plausible as an order-of-magnitude staffing cost, but too imprecise as a consumer pain claim. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a 2024 median of $59,440 for interpreters/translators and $95,410 for speech-language pathologists. Public private-tutor prices observed during this review ranged roughly from $30 to $80 per hour. An interpreter, language teacher, Deaf curriculum consultant, and SLP are different roles. The pain is better expressed as: qualified, individualized support is costly, scarce, and difficult to schedule consistently.

### “A virtual avatar can replace a signer”

Not acceptable as the initial framing. V1 should treat Deaf/ASL experts as authors and validators whose work is distributed through software. The system scales expert-approved material; it does not eliminate expert judgment.

## Most defensible opportunity

There is a credible wedge at the intersection of:

- Chinese dialogic story support for tired caregivers;
- adaptive difficulty based on demonstrated ability rather than age;
- expert-approved ASL-supported storytelling;
- a child-facing character that creates pauses and turn-taking;
- short sessions that transition into physical play;
- lightweight caregiver observations that improve the next story.

The primary near-term outcome should be **completed high-quality interaction loops**, not screen minutes, pages consumed, or claims about IQ.

## Open research questions

- Which ASL vocabulary and combinations best fit an advanced 20–36-month-old hearing child receiving Chinese narration?
- Will parents pay for expert-validated sign content if their child is hearing and already verbal?
- Does the avatar increase child and caregiver conversational turns compared with a normal audiobook or ordinary book?
- Can a 3–5 minute assisted session reliably create 5–10 minutes of offline interaction?
- Does adaptation produce observable gains in spontaneous sign use and story participation?
- Is the child's interest in a virtual cat durable beyond novelty?
