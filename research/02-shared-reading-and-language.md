# Shared reading, language, and realistic dosage

## Professional guidance

The 2024 American Academy of Pediatrics policy recommends supporting reading aloud and shared reading from infancy through at least kindergarten entry. The emphasis is on enjoyable, developmentally appropriate, language-rich interaction and integration into daily routines—not a prescribed number of minutes.

The AAP's HealthyChildren guidance for 18–23-month-olds suggests setting aside **10–15 minutes** with the television off as part of a regular bedtime routine. This is an example, not a hard ceiling or universal dose.

### Product implication

The founder has chosen a 60-minute daily reading journey as a product requirement. Present it transparently as a family goal, not a doctor-mandated dose. Let families reach it cumulatively and preserve a two-minute activation target so that beginning never feels overwhelming. Track meaningful interaction in addition to elapsed time.

When the child is engaged and responding, do not stop merely because a fixed micro-session has ended. Continue with another story beat and adapt upward, sideways, or into a new modality. When attention drops, transition to a physical book, audio-guided play, movement, or a natural stopping point rather than using rewards to force continuation.

## How large are the benefits?

The literature is positive but heterogeneous:

- A meta-analysis of 19 randomized controlled trials (`N=2,594`, ages 1–6) found small-to-moderate improvements in expressive language (`d=0.41`) and receptive language (`d=0.26`), plus a large improvement in caregiver book-sharing competence (`d=1.01`). Lower intervention dosage was associated with minimal child impact.
- A different 2019 meta-analysis estimated a small overall effect (`g=0.194`) and a negligible effect in studies using active control groups (`g=0.028`). Follow-up effects were not statistically significant.
- A meta-analysis of 38 studies and 2,455 children found that shared storybooks support word learning and that dialogic styles moderated results.

These findings do not justify “reading one hour creates X outcome” or a simple linear “more is always better” claim. They support designing for sustained, repeatable, interactive, developmentally matched experiences when the child remains willingly engaged.

## What makes shared reading valuable?

The likely mechanisms include:

- joint attention to the same object or event;
- richer and less common vocabulary than routine talk;
- repetition in a meaningful narrative;
- caregiver responsiveness to the child's focus;
- conversational turn-taking;
- expansions and recasts that add slightly more complex language;
- discussion of causes, emotions, past/future events, and imagined possibilities;
- physical closeness, predictable routine, and positive affect.

The “extra-textual talk” around the book is not a distraction from reading. For toddlers, it is often a central part of the language value.

## Interaction design guidance

The avatar should use this sequence:

1. **Notice:** establish a shared visual focus.
2. **Model:** narrate one short beat in natural Chinese and show one validated sign.
3. **Invite:** create a genuine reason for the child to respond.
4. **Wait:** allow speech, sign, gesture, pointing, or movement.
5. **Acknowledge:** respond to the meaning, not only correctness.
6. **Expand:** add one concept above the child's demonstrated level.
7. **Continue:** let the response change or reveal the next story event.

Avoid turning every page into “这是什么？” testing. Include comments, choices, predictions, role play, surprise, and child-led detours.

## Screens: displacement is the central risk

A meta-analysis of 42 studies (`N=18,905`) found that more total screen time and background television were associated with lower language skills, while educational content and caregiver co-viewing were associated with better language. These are mainly observational associations, not simple proof of causation.

The design response is not merely a timer. Read-for-Xixi should:

- default to caregiver-child co-use;
- keep story beats short;
- pause rather than autoplay;
- provide an audio-first/eyes-away mode after a sign demonstration;
- send the child to find, hide, move, imitate, or show something in the room;
- continue across successful loops while the child remains actively engaged, with parent-controlled stopping;
- avoid infinite feeds, autoplay, streak pressure, or attention-maximizing rewards.
