# American Sign Language with Chinese storytelling

## What the evidence says for hearing toddlers

The best systematic review of baby signs in typically developing hearing children under 36 months located only 10 eligible reports. It found no convincing evidence that deliberately teaching symbolic gestures improves lasting receptive or expressive spoken language. The only study reporting standardized language advantages found no individual-measure differences after 24 months. The review found no evidence of harm to typical development.

For a child who already speaks full Chinese sentences, signs should therefore be framed as:

- an additional visual modality;
- embodied vocabulary and concept play;
- a bridge into a genuine second language if the family sustains natural-language input;
- a storytelling and parent-child interaction medium;

not as a proven spoken-language accelerator.

## Four things that must not be conflated

1. **Natural gesture:** pointing, showing, depicting size or motion.
2. **Baby signs:** selected symbolic gestures paired with speech.
3. **Sign-supported Mandarin:** Mandarin narration/word order with selected signed keywords.
4. **A natural sign language:** a full language such as Chinese Sign Language (CSL), ASL, Hong Kong Sign Language, or Taiwan Sign Language, each with its own lexicon and grammar.

An MVP that narrates Mandarin and inserts selected ASL vocabulary is most accurately called **ASL-supported Chinese storytelling**, unless it later teaches ASL syntax and discourse as a separate language layer.

## Selected product language: ASL

The product decision is to use **American Sign Language vocabulary with Chinese spoken narration**. Read-for-Xixi will not teach Chinese Sign Language.

If Chinese sentences retain Mandarin word order while selected ASL signs appear at key moments, the accurate label is:

> Chinese storytelling supported by expert-validated ASL vocabulary.

Do not claim to teach full ASL grammar unless complete ASL narrative sequences are separately authored and validated by fluent Deaf ASL educators. Chinese Sign Language research remains useful background demonstrating why signed languages cannot be treated as universal or interchangeable, but CSL is outside the MVP curriculum.

### Product terminology

- Use: `ASL-supported Chinese storytelling`.
- Use: `expert-validated ASL vocabulary`.
- Do not use: `Chinese Sign Language`.
- Do not use: `full ASL instruction` unless the product later teaches ASL grammar and narrative directly.

## Curriculum structure for an advanced verbal toddler

Age controls safety, motor expectations, session length, and adult participation. It should not set language difficulty.

Maintain separate dimensions:

- spoken-Chinese comprehension;
- spoken-Chinese expression and narrative complexity;
- sign recognition;
- sign imitation;
- spontaneous sign production;
- sign combinations;
- spatial, emotional, causal, and temporal concepts;
- motor feasibility;
- interests and attention patterns.

A child may receive complex Chinese narration with a much simpler sign target. For example, a story can discuss why Mimi hid under the chair while targeting validated signs for `HIDE`, `UNDER`, and `SCARED`.

## Human validation requirement

Every sign asset must have:

- named language/variety;
- gloss and Chinese meaning;
- intended age/motor adaptation;
- handshape, orientation, location, movement, and nonmanual description;
- provenance and recording consent;
- validation by at least one fluent Deaf ASL signer/educator;
- version and review date;
- known ASL variants where relevant;
- a “do not generate” rule for unvalidated combinations.
